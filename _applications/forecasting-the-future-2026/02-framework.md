## Compute is the new current account

For three centuries, mercantilism meant accumulating gold and running trade surpluses. Modern Mercantilism has a different unit of account. States now hoard, subsidize, restrict and weaponize **productive capability itself**: chips, energy, models, data and the talent that binds them. My central claim is simple: over the next decade, **AI compute and its complements replace the trade balance as the scoreboard of national power**, and nearly every major economic-policy choice of the 2026 to 2036 period can be read as a move on that board.

I write this from Quito, Ecuador. I ran the digital transformation of my country's social security institute, a state institution serving millions in a fully dollarized economy, and I now lead an AI-native firm serving Latin American businesses. That vantage point matters for this challenge because the decade's swing variable is not what Washington or Beijing does. It is what the other six billion people's governments do when both blocs come selling.

## The engine: four forces, one collision

**Force 1: AI is deflationary in output but concentrating in ownership.** Intelligence is becoming cheap; the means of producing it are becoming expensive. Hyperscaler capex guidance for 2026 alone is roughly USD 700 billion, most of it AI-tied. Costs per token collapse while the capital barrier to the frontier rises. This is the classic setup for oligopoly at the core and commodification at the edge.

**Force 2: Mercantilism is inflationary in inputs but distributive in intent.** The US average effective tariff rate sits near its highest level since the 1940s, and industrial policy is now bipartisan in Washington, structural in Beijing, and spreading through the G20. Tariffs, subsidies and localization raise costs on purpose: they buy domestic capability and political resilience with consumer surplus.

**Force 3: The two forces are converging on the same choke points.** Chips, electricity, and data are simultaneously the inputs AI needs most and the instruments mercantilism controls best. The January 2026 US policy combination, loosening some chip export licenses while imposing a 25% tariff on advanced semiconductors, shows the synthesis: trade tools are no longer about protecting industries from imports, they are about **pricing rivals' access to the future**.

**Force 4: Diffusion beats frontier.** The economic returns to AI accrue not to whoever trains the best model but to whoever pushes usable AI deepest into firms, hospitals, tax agencies and farms. Export controls defend the frontier at the cost of diffusion reach; open-weights strategies sacrifice frontier rents to buy global adoption. This is the decade's central trade-off, and China has visibly chosen the diffusion side of it with open-weights releases while the US chooses frontier protection.

The collision of these forces produces what I call the **Compute Settlement**: an emerging, unwritten global bargain in which access to compute and models is exchanged for the things states still uniquely control: energy, minerals, market access, data and alignment.

## The board: a three-tier world

**Tier 1: Compute sovereigns (US bloc and China bloc).** They can train frontier models domestically. Their rivalry is genuine but bounded: both need the other's inputs (rare earths one way, equipment and demand the other), so I expect managed escalation, not rupture. Formal AI arms-control between them stays unlikely this decade (F19, 30%).

**Tier 2: Compute-endowed middle powers (Gulf states, India, EU, Japan, Korea).** They hold something a sovereign needs: energy and capital (Gulf), scale and talent (India), lithography and standards (Europe, Japan, Korea). They convert those endowments into compute through exactly the kind of deals F4 and F9 forecast. The Gulf is executing fastest; I expect a top-ten cluster there by 2028 (75%). India's ambitions outrun its grid and import dependence, which is why I take the contrarian side on it reaching top-three compute this decade (F10, 20%).

**Tier 3: Compute takers (most of the Global South, including my region).** They will not train frontier models. Their choice is which stack to adopt: US commercial, Chinese open-weights, or sovereign-flavored hybrids. Two things follow. First, open-weights models become the developing world's default infrastructure because they are free, good enough and localizable; this is the quiet channel through which the diffusion strategy wins market share regardless of leaderboard rankings (which is why F13 is only 35%: leaderboard dominance is not the prize). Second, the first mass-scale public-sector AI deployments will appear here, where the state is the biggest service provider and the marginal value of automating scarce expertise (doctors, teachers, caseworkers) is highest. Hence F20 at 60%.

Having operated inside a Tier 3 state institution, I can report the binding constraint is not model quality. It is procurement, data plumbing, unions, and political risk tolerance. That is why diffusion into GDP statistics is slower than the technology justifies: I put only 40% on a 2.5% US productivity boom window by 2031 (F15), even while expecting visible white-collar displacement in routine clerical work much sooner (F14, 55%).

## Dynamics to watch: where consensus is most wrong

**1. The capex cycle is a cycle.** Roughly USD 450 billion a year of AI-tied infrastructure spend is being financed against revenues that are still mostly reallocated IT budgets. Depreciation schedules, power constraints and one disappointing capability generation are enough to produce a single down year before 2030. I put 55% on a capex decline year (F11): not a crash call, a cyclicality call against a consensus that currently prices in monotonic growth.

**2. Mercantilism is sticky because it is fiscal.** Tariffs now raise meaningful US revenue, and industrial policy creates constituencies. Whoever wins the next elections inherits both. That is why F1 (tariffs still at or above 10% at end-2027) is 80% and the WTO's appellate paralysis persisting is 85% (F3): restoring the old order would require spending political capital that no major player gains from spending.

**3. Money moves slower than headlines.** De-dollarization talk grows louder every year, and every year the plumbing barely moves. Network effects in reserves and settlement are among the strongest in economics, and, writing from a dollarized country, I would add: credibility is imported precisely when your own institutions are weak, which describes much of the supposed de-dollarization coalition. F17 (35%) and F18 (25%) take the slow side deliberately.

**4. State equity is the new tariff.** The US stake in Intel normalized something that would have been unthinkable in 2020. Golden shares and state stakes in chip and AI champions are cheaper than subsidies and more controlling than tariffs; I expect the practice to spread beyond the US (F5, 60%).

## Falsification: what would change my mind

This framework fails if: (a) a US-China grand bargain rolls tariffs back below 5% and restores the WTO (breaks F1 to F3); (b) AI capability gains stall enough that compute loses its strategic premium (breaks F4, F9, F11's relevance); or (c) frontier models become so efficient that training decentralizes and compute stops being a choke point (breaks the whole scoreboard claim). I assign the joint scenario under 10% this decade, but the appendix flags per-forecast tripwires.

The next decade will not be decided by whether AI is powerful or whether mercantilism persists. Both are already true. It will be decided by the terms of the Compute Settlement: who supplies intelligence, who supplies its inputs, and what the six billion people in the middle get for their alignment. My forecasts are twenty specific, scoreable claims about those terms.
